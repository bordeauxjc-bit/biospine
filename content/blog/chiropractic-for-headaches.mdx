Headache is a symptom, not a single diagnosis. A headache associated with restricted neck movement calls for a different examination than a migraine, a headache after a collision, or sudden severe pain.

At BioSpine, the first question is not “Where should we adjust?” It is “Does this pattern appear to involve the neck, and is chiropractic care appropriate at all?”

## The three headache types we see most often

### Tension-type headaches

The most common kind. They feel like a band of pressure around the head, often worse at the end of the day. The pain is usually moderate, dull, and non-throbbing. Triggers include:

- Prolonged screen time
- Poor posture (forward-head position)
- Neck and shoulder muscle tension
- Stress and jaw clenching
- Dehydration

Neck and shoulder tension can accompany this kind of headache. If the exam finds limited neck motion or tender muscles that reproduce familiar symptoms, Dr. Jordan may discuss manual care, exercise, and changes to the activities that aggravate it.

### Cervicogenic headaches

These headaches are attributed to structures in the neck. They may be one-sided, aggravated by neck movement, and accompanied by reduced range of motion. Those features can suggest a neck contribution, but an examination is still needed before naming the cause.

Conservative care may be reasonable when the examination supports a mechanical neck source and no warning signs are present. Treatment is chosen from the findings rather than from the headache label alone.

### Migraines

Migraines are neurological events, not muscular ones. They typically involve:

- Throbbing pain, often one-sided
- Nausea
- Sensitivity to light or sound
- Sometimes visual "aura" before the headache starts

Migraine management often belongs with a primary-care clinician, neurologist, or headache specialist. A person can have migraine and neck pain at the same time; treating neck stiffness should not be presented as treating the neurological disorder itself.

## What an examination at BioSpine looks for

Dr. Jordan asks about the headache pattern, recent injuries, medications, vision changes, neurological symptoms, sleep, and the movements that make symptoms better or worse. The physical examination may include:

- Neck range of motion and joint movement
- Orthopedic and neurological screening
- Palpation of neck and shoulder muscles
- A review of work, screen, sleep, and activity patterns
- Referral for medical evaluation when the history does not fit conservative neck care

## If the neck appears to be contributing

Care may include a combination of joint mobilization or manipulation, soft-tissue treatment, and exercises selected for the examination findings. Dr. Jordan should be able to explain why each part is being used and what change will be measured.

Evidence for manual therapy varies by headache diagnosis and is not a guarantee for an individual patient. The [American Headache Society’s clinical resources](https://americanheadachesociety.org/resources/clinicians/guidelines) are a useful starting point for evidence-based migraine care.

## How progress is judged

There is no preset number of visits for a headache complaint. Useful measures include headache frequency, intensity, medication use, neck movement, sleep interruption, and missed work or activities. If those measures do not improve, the plan should change or another provider should be involved.

## When to see a different kind of doctor

Not every headache belongs in a chiropractor’s office. **Seek medical evaluation** if your headache:

- Is the "worst headache of your life" and came on suddenly
- Follows a head injury
- Is accompanied by fever, confusion, vision loss, or weakness
- Has changed significantly in character or pattern recently
- Wakes you up at night

These symptoms need prompt medical assessment rather than a routine chiropractic appointment.
