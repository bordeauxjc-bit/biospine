BioSpine offers both [Class IV laser therapy](/services/class-iv-laser-therapy) and [focused shockwave therapy](/services/shockwave-therapy) at its Lake City office. They use different forms of energy, and either treatment needs a clinical reason to be part of your care.

The useful starting question is which findings explain your pain and what improvement you hope to make. A treatment's name alone cannot tell you whether it suits your condition.

## Laser therapy uses light

Therapeutic laser treatment delivers light to a selected area. “Class IV” identifies a laser hazard category. It does not establish that a device treats every painful condition or will produce a better outcome than another treatment.

The [FDA's laser safety guidance](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/laser-products-and-instruments/frequently-asked-questions-about-lasers) describes the eye and skin hazards associated with Class IV lasers. Treatment requires appropriate precautions, including suitable protective eyewear. Sensation depends on the equipment and settings; tell the clinician immediately about uncomfortable heat or pain.

Ask which equipment is proposed, why its use fits your findings, and what limitations apply. The term “cold laser” does not, by itself, identify the equipment or protocol. Our [laser service guide](/services/class-iv-laser-therapy) explains that terminology in more detail.

## Shockwave therapy uses acoustic pulses

Shockwave treatment delivers acoustic energy through an applicator. At BioSpine, the service is focused shockwave therapy. It can feel like repeated tapping or thumping, and the treatment discussion should cover comfort, precautions, and aftercare.

Persistent plantar fascia symptoms are one reason people ask about this treatment. The [AAOS plantar fasciitis guide](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/plantar-fasciitis-and-bone-spurs/) describes mixed results for shockwave therapy, so it should not be presented as a certain cure. Our [heel-pain article](/blog/heel-pain-plantar-fasciitis-shockwave-lake-city) discusses the evaluation and questions to bring to a visit.

## Which one works better?

There is no useful universal winner for every muscle, tendon, or joint complaint. A sore heel, an injured shoulder, and pain spreading from the back are different clinical situations. Findings, previous treatment, health history, and practical goals should guide the choice.

Research on a treatment also has limits. A [systematic review of high-intensity laser therapy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36836014/) found possible improvements in musculoskeletal pain and function, while noting concerns about study bias and evidence quality. Those findings do not establish superiority to shockwave or predict an individual patient's result.

Ask Dr. Jordan to explain the proposed benefit in terms you can measure: walking more comfortably, tolerating a work task, or regaining a particular movement. Pain relief and the ability to resume normal activity should be reassessed during care.

## Before agreeing to a treatment plan

Bring these questions to the appointment:

- What is the working diagnosis, and is more evaluation needed?
- Why is this modality a reasonable option for that diagnosis?
- What alternatives should I consider?
- What are the risks, cost, and proposed visit schedule?
- What should improve, and when will we reassess?
- What home activity or exercise guidance belongs in the plan?

You can ask for an evaluation before deciding on either modality. Explain your symptoms when scheduling rather than feeling you need to choose a machine first.
