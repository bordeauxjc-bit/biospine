Heel pain can turn the walk from the parking lot into the hardest part of a workday. If the first steps after getting out of bed or standing up are especially uncomfortable, plantar fasciitis is one possibility worth discussing at an examination.

BioSpine offers [focused shockwave therapy in Lake City](/services/shockwave-therapy). Choosing it starts with identifying the painful tissue, reviewing what you have already tried, and deciding whether this treatment fits the problem.

## What does plantar fasciitis usually feel like?

The plantar fascia is a band of tissue along the sole that supports the arch. [MedlinePlus describes plantar fasciitis](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007021.htm) as a cause of pain and stiffness at the bottom of the heel, often worse with the first steps after rest. Changes in activity, footwear, and calf or Achilles flexibility can be relevant to the history.

The location matters. Pain behind the heel, pain after a specific injury, or burning and numbness can point toward other problems. Tell the clinician exactly where it hurts and whether you can put weight on the foot comfortably.

A heel spur on an X-ray also does not settle the diagnosis. The [American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons explains](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/plantar-fasciitis-and-bone-spurs/) that many heel spurs are painless and plantar fasciitis can be treated without removing one.

## Where does shockwave therapy fit?

Shockwave therapy uses acoustic pulses applied to a selected area. It is one option to discuss for persistent plantar fascia symptoms after an appropriate assessment and a review of conservative care.

AAOS describes the evidence for plantar fasciitis shockwave treatment as mixed. It is a non-invasive option sometimes considered before surgery, rather than a guaranteed next step for every sore heel. An examination should help decide whether it is reasonable in your case and whether another evaluation is needed first.

Common early approaches include footwear support, appropriate stretching, and changes to aggravating activity. Bring a clear account of what you have tried, how long you tried it, and what changed. That is more useful than simply reporting that “nothing worked.”

## Make the visit specific to your day

Before an appointment, note:

- Whether the worst pain occurs on first steps, during a shift, or afterward
- How long you can stand or walk before changing your activity
- Recent changes in shoes, walking, running, or workload
- Previous imaging, injections, or treatment
- What you most want to return to doing comfortably

Bring the shoes you wear most often if practical. Someone standing through a shift may have different activity goals from someone trying to resume running; both should be discussed when setting a plan.

## Questions to ask before starting treatment

Ask what findings support the diagnosis, why shockwave is being considered, what it costs, and how progress will be assessed. Useful measures might include first-step discomfort or how long you can walk without limping. Discuss when the plan should be reconsidered if those measures do not improve.
