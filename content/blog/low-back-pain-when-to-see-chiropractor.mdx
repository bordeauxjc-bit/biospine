Low back pain can follow one awkward lift, build during a long workweek, or arrive without an obvious cause. Many uncomplicated episodes improve, but the symptom alone cannot tell you whether the source is a muscle, joint, disc, nerve, or something outside the spine.

The practical decision is whether you can keep moving safely, whether the trend is improving, and whether any warning signs require a different kind of care.

## Common causes of low back pain

Most low back pain is **mechanical**, meaning it comes from the muscles, joints, ligaments, or discs of the spine. The most common culprits include:

- **Sprains and strains** from lifting, twisting, or overexertion
- **Disc-related pain** from bulging or herniated discs
- **Facet joint irritation** (the small joints where vertebrae meet)
- **Sciatica**, nerve irritation radiating down a leg
- **Postural pain** from prolonged sitting or standing
- **Deconditioning**, weakness in the muscles that support the spine

These are possibilities, not conclusions you can make from symptoms alone. The history and examination help narrow them down.

## What you can try first

For mild, recent-onset low back pain without warning signs, these measures may help:

1. **Keep moving within tolerance.** Short walks and ordinary activity are generally preferable to prolonged bed rest.
2. **Try heat or a wrapped cold pack** if one feels soothing. Protect the skin and stop if it makes symptoms worse.
3. **Temporarily reduce painful lifting or twisting**, then build activity back as symptoms allow.
4. **Change positions during the day** instead of trying to hold one “perfect” posture.
5. **Choose a sleep position you can tolerate.** A pillow between or under the knees is comfortable for some people, but it is not a requirement.

If the pain is worsening, keeps returning, or is limiting work, sleep, walking, or self-care, an examination is reasonable. [NICE guidance for low back pain](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG59/chapter/Recommendations) also emphasizes self-management, staying active, and using manual therapy only as part of a broader plan that includes exercise.

## Signs you should call a chiropractor

Book an appointment if:

- The pain is not trending better
- You’ve had multiple episodes of the same kind of pain
- Pain is limiting work, sleep, or daily activities
- You’re starting to compensate with poor movement patterns
- You feel stiff or "stuck" in certain positions
- The pain radiates into a buttock, hip, or leg

## Red flags, seek urgent care instead

A small number of symptoms indicate that low back pain may be caused by something more serious. **Go to an emergency room or urgent care**, not a chiropractor, if you have any of these:

- Loss of bladder or bowel control
- Numbness in the groin or inner thighs ("saddle anesthesia")
- Significant, progressive weakness in a leg or foot
- Pain following major trauma (a fall, a car accident)
- Fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss alongside the pain
- History of cancer with new-onset back pain

These are rare, but they’re worth knowing.

## What a chiropractor can do

When the examination supports a mechanical problem and conservative care is appropriate, a plan at BioSpine may involve:

- A thorough exam to identify the source of the pain
- **Spinal adjustments** to restore motion to stiff joints
- **Soft-tissue work** to release guarded muscles
- **Specific home exercises** to build the support muscles your spine needs
- Guidance on work positions, lifting, sleep, and daily movements
