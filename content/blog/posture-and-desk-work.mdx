If you spend most of your day at a computer, your spine is paying a price you probably don&rsquo;t notice — until it starts sending you warning shots. Tight shoulders by 3 PM. A headache that builds all afternoon. Low back stiffness when you stand up. Mid-back tension between your shoulder blades.

These are all the same conversation, and it starts with how you sit.

## What desk work does to your body

The modern workstation creates three predictable patterns:

1. **Forward head posture** — your head drifts forward over your body, multiplying the load on your neck muscles
2. **Rounded shoulders** — chest muscles shorten, upper back muscles stretch and weaken
3. **Anterior pelvic tilt or hip flexor tightness** — eight hours in hip flexion changes how your pelvis sits over your legs

Over months and years, these patterns compound. What started as end-of-day stiffness becomes persistent pain that no amount of stretching fixes.

## The 20-minute rule

The most important thing you can do for your spine during desk work has nothing to do with your chair: **move every 20 minutes**.

Set a timer. When it goes off, do one of these for 30–60 seconds:

- Stand up and walk to the window
- Do 10 shoulder rolls backwards
- Stand and stretch your hip flexors (one knee down, one foot forward, push hips forward)
- Press your chin straight back ("making a double chin") to reverse forward head posture
- Reach overhead and lean side-to-side

This single habit outperforms any ergonomic chair money can buy.

## Setting up your workstation

Once you&rsquo;ve got the movement habit, the setup matters. Here&rsquo;s a no-nonsense checklist:

### Monitor height

- Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level
- Screen roughly an arm&rsquo;s length away
- If you wear progressive lenses, go a notch lower to avoid tipping your chin up

### Chair

- Feet flat on the floor (or on a footrest)
- Hips slightly above knees
- Back of your thighs not pressing into the edge of the seat
- Lumbar support contacting your low back

### Keyboard and mouse

- Elbows at about 90 degrees
- Shoulders relaxed, not shrugging
- Mouse close enough that you&rsquo;re not reaching
- Wrists roughly neutral, not bent up or down

### Phone and tablet

- Never cradle a phone between your ear and shoulder
- Hold tablets up at eye level when reading, not down in your lap

## The best single investment

If you&rsquo;re going to spend money on one thing: **get a sit-stand desk**. The goal isn&rsquo;t to stand all day — that causes its own problems — it&rsquo;s to change positions throughout the day. Most people do best with 30–60 minute cycles.

## Three exercises to undo desk damage

Do these at the end of the workday:

### 1. Doorway chest stretch
Stand in a doorway, place your forearm against the frame with your elbow at 90 degrees, and step through. Hold 30 seconds per side.

### 2. Prone Y
Lie face-down. Lift your arms off the floor in a Y shape, squeezing your shoulder blades together and down. 10 slow reps.

### 3. Hip flexor stretch
From a half-kneeling position (one knee down), tuck your pelvis and push your hips gently forward. You should feel it in the front of the hip of the kneeling leg. 30 seconds per side.

Three exercises. Five minutes. Done daily, they&rsquo;ll do more for your long-term spinal health than most expensive interventions.
